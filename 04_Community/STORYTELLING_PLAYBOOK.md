# STORYTELLING_PLAYBOOK.md

> *People rarely rally around engineering. They rally around possibility.*

---

# Purpose

This playbook is for everyone speaking publicly about Lid I-5 North.

Whether you're talking to a neighbor over coffee, speaking at a community meeting, meeting with a City Councilmember, or giving a presentation to a civic organization, the objective is the same:

**Help people imagine a better Seattle.**

We are not trying to "win an argument."

We are inviting people to join a long-term civic project.

---

# Our North Star

If people remember only one thing, let it be this:

> **For fifty years, I-5 has divided our neighborhoods. We now have an opportunity to reconnect them for the next hundred.**

Everything else supports that idea.

---

# The Conversation Pyramid

Most conversations should move in this order.

## 1. Begin with Values

People.

Neighborhoods.

Walking.

Businesses.

Children.

Community.

Identity.

Never begin with engineering.

---

## 2. Tell a Story

Stories create emotional context.

Facts give credibility.

Facts alone rarely inspire action.

---

## 3. Add Evidence

Once people understand the vision, introduce:

- examples from Seattle
- successful projects elsewhere
- economics
- public health
- transportation
- environmental benefits

---

## 4. Invite Participation

Always end with

> "What would you like to see here?"

People remember conversations they helped create.

# The 30-Second Elevator Pitch

Wallingford and the University District are close enough to be one connected community, but I-5 makes them feel far apart.

Our vision is to turn that divide into a welcoming seam: safer and more comfortable ways to walk, bike, and cross; new parks and gathering places; and stronger connections among homes, businesses, schools, and transit.

Covering parts of the freeway may help make that possible, but the lid is not the whole project. The project is about reconnecting neighborhoods and creating a place that serves the community for generations.

---

# The One-Minute Version

I-5 is essential regional infrastructure, but in North Seattle it also divides neighborhoods that should feel naturally connected. Wallingford and the University District are only a short distance apart, yet the freeway, ramps, noise, and difficult crossings make everyday movement between them uncomfortable.

Our vision is larger than putting a lid over the freeway. We want to create a connected district—a welcoming seam between neighborhoods—with safer walking and biking routes, better access to transit and local businesses, new parks and civic spaces, and opportunities for housing and community uses where they make sense.

A freeway lid is one tool. Improved bridges, streets, crossings, and thoughtfully planned development may be others. Together, they can preserve regional mobility while repairing the local urban fabric.

This is not about hiding a freeway. It is about shaping the next generation of community life around it.

## The Problem

The neighborhoods around NE 45th Street are some of Seattle's most vibrant.

Wallingford.

The University District.

The Burke-Gilman Trail.

Local businesses.

Schools.

Parks.

But they're separated by one of the widest freeways in the Northwest.

Crossing I-5 is something we tolerate, not something we enjoy.

---

## The Opportunity

Around the world, cities are beginning to reconnect neighborhoods divided by freeways.

Seattle has already done this in places:

Freeway Park.

The Montlake Lid.

The SR-99 waterfront transformation.

Now we have another opportunity.

---

## The Vision

Imagine walking across a green park instead of a freeway bridge.

Imagine children riding bicycles comfortably.

Imagine local businesses benefiting from people walking instead of driving past.

Imagine community events that naturally bring Wallingford and the University District together.

That's the opportunity.

---

## Closing

The question isn't simply whether we can build a lid.

The real question is:

**What kind of Seattle do we want to build together?**

# Frequently Asked Questions

## Isn't this just an expensive park?

No.

Parks are one outcome.

The larger objectives are reconnecting neighborhoods, improving walking and cycling, reducing noise, strengthening local businesses, creating development opportunities, improving public health, and expanding civic space.

---

## Why here?

Because this is where two vibrant neighborhoods almost touch.

The freeway is the barrier.

The lid becomes the connection.

---

## Why now?

Seattle is growing.

Infrastructure lasts generations.

The decisions we make now will shape the city for the next century.

---

## Won't this make traffic worse?

The lid itself doesn't remove freeway lanes.

The goal is to improve what happens above the freeway while preserving regional mobility below.

---

## Isn't Seattle facing bigger problems?

Yes.

Housing.

Climate.

Public safety.

Transportation.

Economic development.

The strength of this project is that it contributes to many of these goals simultaneously rather than addressing only one.

---

## Why not spend the money somewhere else?

Cities need both maintenance and vision.

Major civic investments often produce benefits that extend well beyond the project itself.

Great public spaces strengthen neighborhoods for generations.

---

# Common Objections

## "It's unrealistic."

Response

Many of Seattle's defining civic places once seemed unrealistic.

Freeway Park.

The Pike Place Market preservation effort.

The SR-99 tunnel and waterfront.

The question is not whether ambitious projects are difficult.

They are.

The question is whether they're worthwhile.

---

## "It will never be funded."

Response

Large civic projects are almost always financed through multiple sources over many years.

The first step is not funding.

The first step is building community consensus around a compelling vision.

Funding follows credible projects with broad support.

---

## "The city has other priorities."

Response

Exactly.

The most successful projects solve several priorities at once.

Housing.

Mobility.

Climate.

Economic development.

Public health.

Neighborhood vitality.

That makes them more—not less—competitive.

---

## "Nobody will use it."

Response

Look at any successful public space in Seattle.

People naturally gather where walking is pleasant, businesses are active, and public spaces feel welcoming.

If we create a great place, people will use it.

# Historical Stories Worth Telling

People remember stories better than timelines.

Keep these short.

### The Freeway

In the 1960s, I-5 helped move a growing region.

It also divided neighborhoods that had evolved together over decades.

Today's challenge is different.

We can preserve mobility while repairing those connections.

---

### The Streetcars

Before I-5, neighborhoods were connected by streetcars, local businesses, and walkable streets.

The lid isn't about recreating the past.

It's about restoring the ease of connection that once defined the area.

---

### Seattle's Civic Tradition

Seattle has repeatedly invested in ambitious public places.

Pike Place Market.

Gas Works Park.

Freeway Park.

The waterfront.

Each required imagination before construction.

# Speaking Style

Conversations should feel optimistic.

Not promotional.

Curious rather than argumentative.

Avoid exaggeration.

If we don't know something, say so.

People trust honesty.

---

# Our Tone

We don't argue that Seattle made a mistake.

The freeway reflected the priorities of its time.

Seattle's priorities have evolved.

Cities evolve too.

---

# The Closing Question

Rather than ending with a statement, end with a question.

"What would make you excited to walk across I-5?"

or

"What would you hope your grandchildren experience here that isn't possible today?"

Those questions invite imagination.

And imagination is where civic projects begin.

# Final Thought

Facts matter.

Engineering matters.

Economics matter.

But history suggests that transformative civic projects begin somewhere much simpler.

Someone helps another person imagine a future that feels both inspiring and achievable.

Our job is to start that conversation—and then keep inviting more people into it.
