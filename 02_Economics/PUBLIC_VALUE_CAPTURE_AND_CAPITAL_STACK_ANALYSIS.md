# PUBLIC_VALUE_CAPTURE_AND_CAPITAL_STACK_ANALYSIS.md
## Preliminary analysis for Lid I-5 North
### Public value, land value, and how the project could be made fundable

*Prepared for the Lid I-5 North project*  
*June 2026*

---

# 1. Purpose

This memo is a preliminary analysis of how **public value capture** and a **layered capital stack** could support Lid I-5 North.

It builds from the current economics, real estate, and funding documents, especially:

- `ECONOMICS_AND_FUNDING_v2.md`
- `PRELIMINARY_REAL_ESTATE_ANALYSIS.md`
- `CAPITAL_STACK_AND_IMPLEMENTATION_PATHWAYS.md`
- `SHARED_PURPOSE_WITH_LIDI5.md`

This is not an appraisal, legal opinion, pro forma, or engineering estimate. It is a working framework for understanding how the project might convert public infrastructure investment into public benefits, development value, and fundable components.

---

# 2. Core Finding

Lid I-5 North should not be framed as a park that happens to sit over a freeway, or as a real estate project with some public space attached.

The stronger frame is:

> **Lid I-5 North is a public-repair project that creates land value, mobility value, environmental value, and civic value. The capital stack should capture some of that value without allowing the funding strategy to overwhelm the public mission.**

The project will almost certainly require a blended capital stack. No single source is likely to fund it. The most realistic strategy is to combine:

- federal barrier-repair and multimodal grants;
- state/WSDOT participation;
- Seattle capital funding and local match;
- philanthropy for public-space quality;
- development value from buildable land or air rights;
- selective local value capture, such as a Local Improvement District or tax increment tool, only if legal and political analysis supports it.

# 3. Working Land-Area Assumptions

Current planning discussions have used the following rough program:

| Use | Acres | Approx. square feet | Planning meaning |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Total lid area | 22 acres | 958,320 sf | Overall lid planning envelope |
| Buildable development parcels | 6 acres | 261,360 sf | Potential value-capture engine |
| Roads / circulation / access | 3 acres | 130,680 sf | Mobility and service framework |
| Open space / park / civic realm | 13 acres | 566,280 sf | Core public-benefit area |

These are conceptual numbers. They should be updated once engineering, span limits, ramps, grades, ventilation, fire/life-safety, WSDOT requirements, and urban design alternatives are better understood.

The key point is that the project has both a **large public-space component** and a **meaningful development-value component**. The 6 buildable acres are not enough to pay for the whole project, but they are large enough to matter.

---

# 4. Public Value Created by the Lid

The project creates public value in several ways.

## 4.1 Reconnection value

The lid can repair a freeway barrier between Wallingford, the University District, Green Lake, Roosevelt, and Ravenna. Better crossings at NE 45th and NE 50th would make nearby neighborhoods function more like one connected urban district.

## 4.2 Mobility and safety value

The project can improve walking, biking, transit access, and local circulation across I-5. This is important for federal and state funding because it frames the lid as transportation repair, not only open space.

## 4.3 Environmental and public-health value

The lid can add tree canopy, stormwater features, shade, open space, and potential noise buffering. Air quality benefits should be studied carefully; they should not be overstated before ventilation and pollutant-dispersion analysis.

## 4.4 Economic and business-district value

The lid can turn the freeway edge from a weak commercial seam into an active connector between Wallingford and the University District. Better foot traffic, safer crossings, and new public-space frontage could support neighborhood-serving retail, kiosks, food uses, services, events, and small business activity.

## 4.5 Development and land value

The lid may create new buildable parcels or improve the value of existing freeway-edge parcels. This is the most direct value-capture opportunity, especially if the public sector can retain control through ground leases or air-rights agreements.

# 5. Real Estate Context

The study area is not one real estate market. It is two different markets separated by I-5.

## East of I-5

The Roosevelt / University District side is a high-intensity transit-oriented development market. It includes Seattle Mixed-University zoning, major height allowances, strong development pressure, and proximity to the U District light rail station.

The lid would likely enhance an already strong market by improving westward access, public realm, and neighborhood image.

## West of I-5

The Latona / Wallingford side is primarily lower-scale residential, with NR3, LR3, and a finer-grained neighborhood pattern. Residential values are already strong, but current zoning does not fully capture proximity to the U District.

The lid may create a larger relative value change on the west side by reducing the freeway-edge penalty and improving access to U District transit, jobs, retail, and services.

## Strategic value zones

The strongest value-capture geography is likely to be:

- freeway-edge parcels near NE 45th;
- freeway-edge parcels near NE 50th;
- west-side feeder blocks along or near 5th Ave NE;
- east-side station-area parcels that gain from stronger westward pedestrian catchment;
- any new buildable pads created directly by the lid.

The project should model value by zone, not by applying one uniform uplift assumption across the whole area.

# 6. Development Capacity Sensitivity

The 6 buildable acres equal roughly **261,360 square feet of land**.

Depending on structural limits, zoning, market demand, and urban design choices, that land could support very different development programs.

| Illustrative FAR on 6 acres | Approx. gross development capacity |
|---:|---:|
| 2.0 FAR | 522,720 gsf |
| 4.0 FAR | 1,045,440 gsf |
| 6.0 FAR | 1,568,160 gsf |
| 8.0 FAR | 2,090,880 gsf |

These are not recommendations. They are order-of-magnitude tests.

The actual capacity may be lower if:

- portions of the 6 acres are not structurally buildable;
- ramp geometry or clearance requirements reduce parcel efficiency;
- open-space or circulation needs reduce development footprints;
- parking, servicing, loading, ventilation, or fire access limits development;
- the project chooses lower-scale development to protect neighborhood fit.

The actual value may be higher if:

- development pads are located at strong nodes;
- ground-floor uses activate the lid;
- zoning supports mixed-use density;
- public ownership allows long-term lease capture;
- adjacent parcels are coordinated into a larger redevelopment strategy.

# 7. Preliminary Value-Capture Tools

## 7.1 Ground lease / air-rights development

This is likely the strongest project-specific value-capture tool.

If the lid creates buildable parcels, the project should study long-term ground leases rather than immediate land sales. Ground leases can:

- generate upfront or recurring revenue;
- preserve public control;
- support affordability or community-serving use requirements;
- dedicate some revenue to operations and maintenance;
- avoid selling away long-term public upside too early.

## 7.2 Development contributions from added entitlement

If selected parcels receive additional development capacity because of the lid, some of that incremental value could be captured through negotiated public benefits, development agreements, infrastructure contributions, affordable housing commitments, or public-realm obligations.

This should be targeted. Blanket upzoning of the west side is likely less defensible than a focused strategy around lid portals, mixed-use nodes, and freeway-edge transition areas.

## 7.3 Local Improvement District or special assessment

A Local Improvement District may be plausible if the lid creates a defensible special benefit for nearby properties.

This is not a general tax. The benefit case would need to be carefully established through:

- a benefit district boundary;
- parcel-level special-benefit analysis;
- legal review;
- property-owner engagement;
- a politically realistic assessment structure.

This tool should be treated as optional gap-closing capacity, not the base funding assumption.

## 7.4 Tax increment financing / local increment tools

Washington's tax increment tools are more constrained than the classic TIF models used in some other states. TIF may still help if the City can define an increment area around eligible public improvements and likely redevelopment.

The project needs a project-specific TIF memo before relying on this tool. Key questions include:

- What increment area is legally and politically plausible?
- What public improvements are eligible?
- How much assessed-value growth is realistically attributable to the lid?
- What taxing districts are affected?
- How soon would revenue materialize relative to project debt?

## 7.5 Commercial and operating revenue

Commercial rents, kiosk fees, concessions, events, sponsorships, and programming revenue are unlikely to fund the structure. They may, however, support long-term operations, maintenance, activation, and stewardship.

This revenue should be treated as an operating tool, not a heroic capital source.

# 8. Capital Stack Framework

The capital stack should be organized around what each source is actually paying for.

| Funding source | Best use | Role in stack |
|---|---|---|
| Federal reconnecting communities / multimodal grants | Barrier repair, access, safety, planning, capital construction | Public backbone |
| State / WSDOT participation | Interstate interface, safety, project development, state-local partnership | Necessary partner |
| City of Seattle capital | Local match, parks, streets, public realm, project management | Civic commitment |
| Philanthropy | Signature park, play, cultural, family, and public-space elements | Quality enhancer |
| Ground lease / air rights | Buildable pads, development-enabling infrastructure, operations reserve | Project-specific value engine |
| LID / special assessment | Special-benefit capture from nearby parcels | Optional gap closer |
| TIF / local increment | Long-term taxable value created by infrastructure | Possible supplement |
| Commercial revenue | Kiosks, storefronts, concessions, events | Operations and activation |

The base case should assume that **public funding pays for the public core**, while value capture helps reduce the gap and sustain operations.

# 9. Illustrative Stack Scenarios

These are strategic scenarios, not budgets.

## Scenario A: Public-space-first lid

Primary sources:

- federal grants;
- state participation;
- City capital;
- philanthropy.

Development value is limited. This scenario may be politically cleaner but places more pressure on public and philanthropic funding.

## Scenario B: Hybrid public-space and mixed-use lid

Primary sources:

- federal and state funding for barrier repair and mobility;
- City capital for public realm and match;
- ground lease or air-rights revenue from buildable pads;
- targeted philanthropy;
- possible LID/TIF if legal analysis supports it.

This is the most credible current posture. It treats development value as meaningful, but not dominant.

## Scenario C: Development-led lid

Primary sources:

- aggressive air-rights value;
- major private development participation;
- TIF or LID;
- public funding for core infrastructure.

This could reduce the public funding gap, but it is politically and programmatically risky. If pushed too far, it could weaken the civic-repair narrative and make the project feel like real estate first, community repair second.

---

# 10. Recommended Preliminary Position

The project should proceed under **Scenario B: hybrid public-space and mixed-use lid**.

That means:

- lead with reconnection, safety, public health, open space, and neighborhood repair;
- preserve enough development capacity to create real value;
- use ground leases where possible to retain public control;
- design commercial nodes at NE 45th and NE 50th as part of the public realm;
- treat LID/TIF as serious but unproven tools;
- build a capital stack around fundable components rather than one giant project number.

The right phrase is:

> **public project with development leverage**

not:

> development project with public benefits.

# 11. Public Value Capture Principles

## Principle 1: Capture value without losing the mission

The project should capture land and development value only to the extent that it supports the public purpose: reconnection, public space, safety, health, equity, and neighborhood vitality.

## Principle 2: Keep ownership where long-term public control matters

Where the lid creates valuable development pads, public ownership with long-term ground leases may be better than selling land outright.

## Principle 3: Dedicate some recurring value to operations

A lid is a long-term asset. Lease revenue, commercial revenue, sponsorships, or district revenue should be evaluated not only for capital repayment but also for maintenance, programming, and lifecycle reserves.

## Principle 4: Match the tool to the benefit

Federal grants should pay for barrier repair and mobility. State funds should pay for interstate interface and transportation value. City funds should pay for local civic value. Philanthropy should pay for visible public-space quality. Value capture should pay for value created by the project.

## Principle 5: Make the benefit geography explicit

The project should define benefit zones around the lid portals, adjacent development parcels, and new public-space frontage. A vague claim that "property values will rise" is not enough.

# 12. Early Financial Questions to Answer

The next phase should answer these questions before the project relies on value capture:

1. **How much of the 6 buildable acres is truly buildable?**
2. **What load levels can the lid support, and where?**
3. **What development program is structurally, politically, and financially plausible?**
4. **What is the residual land value of the development pads under multiple scenarios?**
5. **Can the project retain public ownership and ground lease the pads?**
6. **What parcels receive measurable special benefit from the lid?**
7. **Could a LID boundary be legally and politically defensible?**
8. **Could a TIF or increment area produce meaningful revenue within the project timeline?**
9. **What revenue should be reserved for operations and lifecycle costs?**
10. **What public benefits must be required if private development participates?**

# 14. Bottom Line

The Lid I-5 North value-capture story is promising, but it should be handled carefully.

The project sits between two strong but different real estate environments. It could create new public land, new development pads, stronger commercial edges, better access, and measurable public benefits. Those outcomes can support a value-capture strategy.

But value capture will not replace the need for public funding. The lid should be funded as a public infrastructure and civic-repair project, with development value used to close gaps, improve quality, and sustain operations.

The practical goal is to make the project fundable by showing how each source participates:

- federal funding for barrier repair;
- state participation for the I-5 interface;
- City funding for civic and local infrastructure value;
- philanthropy for visible public-space quality;
- ground lease and air-rights value for development-enabled upside;
- LID/TIF only where the legal and benefit case is real.

If the project can make that case clearly, Lid I-5 North can move from a compelling idea to a financeable public project.
